Ask prominent voices on AI for their P(doom) and the answers run from about zero to above 90%. This post is about why the numbers spread so far, and how to read one when you hear it. For what the term means, where it came from and a sourced table of what thought leaders have said, see [the P(doom) page](/p-doom).

## What the number is, and what it isn’t

<Definition />

The P stands for probability, so P(doom) reads as “the probability of doom.” When someone asks “what’s your P(doom)?”, they are asking how worried you are about AI, and asking you to put the answer as a number.

That makes it a useful conversation starter and a bad conversation ender. A single number hides most of what matters: which outcome you mean, over what time frame, and why you believe it.

A P(doom) is a subjective probability. It is a statement of belief, closer to a forecaster’s judgment than to a measurement. There is no record of past AI catastrophes to count, so nobody can check a P(doom) the way you can check a weather forecast.

Some of that spread is real disagreement, about how quickly capabilities will grow, how hard it is to control systems more capable than us, and whether institutions can keep up. But a lot of it comes from people answering different questions:

- **Which outcome?** Human extinction, permanent loss of control, a collapse of civilization, or “things go really badly” are very different events.
- **By when?** “By 2040”, “in the next 30 years” and “ever” give different numbers for the same worldview.
- **Under what assumptions?** Some estimates assume today’s trajectory continues; others assume a serious safety effort or no regulation at all.

Even the same number can hide different reasons. Two people can both say 10%: one because they think alignment is hard but governments will act in time, the other because they doubt that very capable AI is coming soon at all.

And a P(doom) is not the same as being optimistic or pessimistic overall. Plenty of people expect enormous benefits from AI and still put real odds on disaster. Others expect little change either way.

## How to read someone’s P(doom)

When you hear a number, the useful follow-up questions are:

1. Doom meaning what, exactly?
2. By when?
3. Assuming what about safety work and regulation?
4. What would move the number up or down?

A number that comes with those answers tells you far more than the number alone. It also makes disagreements easier to locate: often two people agree about the facts and differ on one assumption.
