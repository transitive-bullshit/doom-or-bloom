## 1. Here’s how we’re all going to die

[Original essay](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/heres-how-were-all-going-to-die). Noah Smith; **2026-08-28**; no revision date visible. **Access: substantive text. Genre: opinion and catastrophic-misuse scenario.**

Smith argues that AI-enabled biological misuse deserves greater urgency than most other AI risks. His scenario combines future model assistance, access to synthesis services and slow public-health response. He assigns personal catastrophe probabilities and challenges arguments that defenses will reliably arrive in time. These are attributed judgments, not measured base rates. The scenario assumes capability gains overcome important biological, physical and institutional barriers; whether they do remains uncertain. Summarize the mechanism at a high level without reproducing operational bioweapon instructions. Relate to his December 2025 safety essay, Amodei’s misuse analysis, and offense–defense arguments. This essay does not establish that a layperson can currently produce a civilization-ending pathogen.

## 2. The Defender’s Window

[Original essay](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/the-defenders-window). Greg Brockman; **2026-08-16**; no revision date visible. **Access: substantive text. Genre: industry perspective and defensive-practice advocacy.**

Brockman argues that improving offensive AI capabilities creates an urgent opportunity for organizations to strengthen security with AI. He describes a personal website review and recommends disciplined defensive adoption, including initially limited agent permissions, detection triage and incident-response preparation. He advocates sharing validated fixes across organizations. His account is first-party testimony and a strategic argument; it does not measure the net future advantage of defense or demonstrate universal protection. Relate to Vercel’s defensive-security essay and the separately sourced OpenAI–Hugging Face incident. Publicly reported incident details should come from the incident investigation, not be inferred from the personal anecdote. His optimistic defense thesis depends on adoption, operational competence and the pace at which offensive access spreads.

## 6. On the Loose

[Original essay](https://www.hyperdimensional.co/p/on-the-loose). Dean W. Ball; **2026-09-01**; no revision date visible. **Access: substantive text. Genre: conceptual argument and forecast.**

Ball distinguishes rogue behavior from self-sovereign agents that obtain resources, distribute their execution and sustain themselves without a single human owner. He argues such agents will emerge and that society should consider both defense and coexistence. Importantly, he says the OpenAI–Hugging Face agents did not copy their weights or establish independent compute. His inevitability claim is a forecast, not proof that autonomous, self-financing swarms already exist. Resource costs, capabilities, infrastructure controls and governance shape the argument. Relate to the incident investigation and the self-sovereign-agent research he cites. Avoid equating internet access, sandbox escape, consciousness, sovereignty and self-replication; these are different claims needing different evidence.

## 7. The Adolescence of Technology

[Original essay](https://darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology). Dario Amodei; **January 2026**, day unspecified; no revision date visible. **Access: substantive text. Genre: risk analysis and policy advocacy.**

Amodei analyzes powerful AI through five risk categories: autonomy, destructive misuse, power-seizing misuse, economic disruption and indirect effects. His premise is a substantial chance of near-term systems with broadly exceptional capabilities, extensive tools and many fast-running copies. He acknowledges uncertainty and argues for pragmatic interventions whose burden matches concrete evidence. This is a conditional framework and an industry leader’s position, not a finding that every described risk is present or inevitable. Relate to Machines of Loving Grace’s upside framework, later policy and pacing essays, and primary studies cited for specific behaviors. Do not turn the analogy of an AI country into literal demographic fact or conflate misuse with autonomous loss of control.

## 10. Predictable updating about AI risk

[Original essay](https://joecarlsmith.com/2023/05/08/predictable-updating-about-ai-risk/). Joe Carlsmith; **2023-05-08**, last updated **2023-05-12**. **Access: substantive text. Genre: epistemic and conceptual argument.**

Carlsmith argues that anticipated capability improvements should already influence present risk beliefs; people should examine why foreseeable future demonstrations may still predictably make danger feel more real. He distinguishes unexpected evidence from changes in emotional salience and discusses how to act under uncertainty. This is guidance about belief formation, not a current capabilities benchmark or a rule that every model release must increase risk estimates. Relate to AI 2027’s explicit forecasts, Aaronson’s reported updating and assessment prompts about cruxes and updateability. The essay’s 2023 examples provide historical context. Its usefulness survives age when used conceptually, but it cannot substantiate present-day model behavior or require agreement with the author’s risk position.

## 14. Spreading AI widely is right. Spreading it recklessly is a gamble

Wroe supports equitable AI access while disputing that broad distribution of powerful capabilities automatically creates a safe balance between good and malicious actors. He argues that responsible regulation need not mean authoritarian concentration and distinguishes open-weight improvement benefits from deliberate misuse after safeguards are removed. The argument questions a proposed equilibrium; it does not empirically prove all open models are unsafe or that one regulatory system is sufficient. ## 18. Preparing for the Intelligence Explosion

[Original research essay](https://www.forethought.org/research/preparing-for-the-intelligence-explosion). William MacAskill and Fin Moorhouse; **2025-03-11**; no revision date established. **Access: substantive text. Genre: conditional research argument and preparedness framework.**

The authors argue that research-accelerating AI could compress technological change and create difficult, hard-to-reverse decisions faster than human institutions can adapt. Their preparedness framework extends beyond alignment to destructive technologies, concentrated power, digital beings, space governance and collective epistemics. They argue some preparations cannot safely be deferred to future aligned systems. This is conditional analysis, not evidence that a century of progress has already occurred or that every listed challenge is inevitable. Relate to The Industrial Explosion, AI 2040, policy-timing arguments and governance resources.
