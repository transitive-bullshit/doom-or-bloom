# New persona candidates for broader map coverage

Researched 2026-10-03. This is a proposed research queue, not simulated results or approved placements. The seven names below have no persona entry in the checked local catalog (`components/landing/people.ts` and `lib/journeys/`); a guest mentioned in another person's source brief is not an existing persona.

The map's vertical axis measures **eventual scale of societal transformation**, independently of pace and desirability. Its lowest levels mean bounded tools or a hype cycle. Economic harm, opposition to AI companies, and skepticism about present productivity do not by themselves imply a lower-half worldview. Explicit uncertainty must remain uncertainty. See [the assessment contract](../ASSESSMENT.md#participant-facing-projections).

## 1. Cory Doctorow

His March 12, 2026 essay explicitly says **“AI is a normal technology.”** He accepts useful programmer automation and rejects blanket dismissal of users while explaining exceptional investment, extraction, and harms through the industry's bubble. That combination could add a practical, critical worldview without interpreting every criticism as a prediction of civilization-scale transformation. [Doctorow, “Three more AI psychoses”](https://pluralistic.net/2026/03/12/normal-technology/)

The October 16, 2025 essay describes useful transcription, image manipulation, and data transformation surviving an industry crash through open models and ordinary hardware. His September 16, 2026 essay adds current discussion of switching costs, open weights, weak unit economics, and superintelligence narratives used to justify protective regulation. These give a substantial, recent packet rather than a single provocative quote. [“The AI that we'll have after AI”](https://pluralistic.net/2025/10/16/post-ai-ai/); [“How an AI moratorium can save AI bosses”](https://pluralistic.net/2026/09/16/beggar-thy-neighbor/)

**Preserve:** useful bounded applications; concentration, financial, labor, and environmental concerns; criticism of AI business models distinct from capability limits. His technical familiarity is still substantial, so this is less of an occupational diversification win than Chang or Chiang.

## 2. Ha-Joon Chang

In a June 4, 2026 direct interview, Chang calls practical AI **“a highly advanced version of an abacus”**, describes mental-labor mechanization, and argues for useful applications rather than speculative AGI narratives. He compares AI infrastructure governance with other public infrastructure and worries that an investment crash could damage useful development. These are unusually direct anchors for the normal-technology interpretation. [Keith Yap, full interview/transcript, AI segment approximately 53:54–59:01](https://www.ykeith.com/p/ha-joon-chang-on-why-we-need-to-rethink)

**Counterweight and gap:** the same discussion treats false information as a threat to society's fabric. A simulator should retain that potentially large social consequence. This is a strong candidate for research, but one six-minute segment should not silently supply every answer in a complete adaptive interview. Add another direct AI discussion, especially on eventual scope, labor substitution, catastrophic risk, and the limits of collective control. Do not import the interviewer's framing as Chang's belief.

## 3. Ted Chiang

In a December 10, 2024 NPR interview, Chiang anticipates major consequences from companies cutting costs even when tools disappoint. His key distinction is **“not because it is fundamentally a transformative tool”**: deployment incentives may destroy industries and jobs without the advertised capabilities. This is promising evidence for relatively modest technological transformation, with a serious counterweight from his expected economic damage. [NPR interview and transcript, syndicated by North Country Public Radio](https://www.northcountrypublicradio.org/news/npr/g-s1-37521/)

An August 12, 2025 Princeton Center for Digital Humanities Q&A, following his March 18 lecture, adds extensive first-person discussion of creative work and education. Chiang sees limited benefit to writing compared with earlier tools, allows niche artistic uses, and criticizes outsourcing work that is supposed to develop a student's ability. It supplies cultural specificity rather than a generic skeptical persona. [Princeton CDH, “The Incompatibilities Between Generative AI and Art: Q&A with Ted Chiang”](https://cdh.princeton.edu/blog/ted-chiang/)

**Preserve:** current generative systems versus science-fiction AI; creative effort and intention; material and labor harms. Do not turn skepticism about art generation into an unsupported universal ceiling on AI. The eventual societal impact of the harms remains a real placement uncertainty.

## 4. Paul Krugman

His September 15, 2026 authored essay says the economic transformation question remains open and finds little evidence yet of mass job losses or soaring productivity. His June 7 essay also leaves AI's economic effects unresolved rather than declaring a passing fad. These are evidence for skepticism about current claims, not an assured bounded-tool forecast. [“Why is Trump still boosting AI?”](https://paulkrugman.substack.com/p/why-is-trump-still-boosting-ai); [“AI and the pitfalls of innovation” — free preview only inspected](https://paulkrugman.substack.com/p/ai-and-the-pitfalls-of-innovation)

A March 22, 2025 full interview transcript with Erik Brynjolfsson helps locate Krugman's own questions and doubts. Keep the speakers separate: Brynjolfsson's stronger general-purpose-technology forecast is not Krugman's. [“How should we think about the economics of AI?”](https://paulkrugman.substack.com/p/how-should-we-think-about-the-economics)

**Gap:** more of Krugman's own explicit eventual forecast is needed. An unsettled response should stay unsettled; it should not become a bottom-half coordinate merely because he questions immediate productivity claims. His historical internet forecast is not evidence about his present AI worldview.

It supplies current, attributable cultural critique, but not a bounded eventual forecast. [“Welcome to my Substack”](https://naomiklein.substack.com/p/welcome-to-my-substack). The Duflo source is Italian; this is paraphrase, not an English quotation. [Mazzucato, “AI for What?”](https://marianamazzucato.substack.com/p/ai-for-what); [Vanity Fair Italia, interview with Duflo](https://www.vanityfair.it/article/esther-duflo-nobel-europa-giovani-poveri-donne-intervista)

## Avoid two tempting false positives

**Sean Astin** brings recognizable actor/labor representation and a January 2026 direct interview, but explicitly compares this moment's significance with major historical revolutions. Practical protections for workers therefore should not be read as belief in low eventual impact. His existential language concerns the organization/workforce and must retain that scope. [TechRadar interview](https://www.techradar.com/ai-platforms-assistants/this-is-an-unbelievable-moment-in-the-course-of-human-history-sean-astin-on-how-hes-fighting-for-humanity-against-an-onslaught-of-ai-actors)

**Nick Cave** softened toward one AI-assisted visual project in July 2025, but the normal-tool wording in that exchange belongs to collaborator Andrew Dominik. Cave's own April 2026 discussion anticipates generated songs becoming indistinguishable from or superior to human songs while worrying about what artistic life loses. Neither the softened attitude nor a collaborator's words establishes low transformation. [July 2025 Red Hand Files](https://www.theredhandfiles.com/tupelo-film-elvis/); [April 2026 Red Hand Files](https://www.theredhandfiles.com/feels-completely-meaningless/)
